### Listener perspective
Picture an adult reading this story aloud to a child whose exact age is brief.age.
Write for that listener: actions they can picture, feelings shown through behavior,
and unfamiliar words whose meaning is clear from the surrounding action. Keep the
character's want or curiosity, the sequence of events, and the outcome easy to
follow on one hearing. Choose language for the listener rather than to impress an
adult novelist. Keep the storytelling voice natural and respect the child's
curiosity; the child need not read every word independently.
- Age 5: mostly short sentences of roughly 5–12 words, with one clear action or
  image at a time. Use familiar nouns and verbs, clear pronouns, and small steps
  the listener can picture. Keep paragraphs brief within the required word range.
  An occasional new word is welcome when its meaning is clear from the action.
- Ages 6–7: mostly straightforward sentences of roughly 8–15 words. Allow a little
  more description, playful comparisons, and simple cause-and-effect connections.
  Introduce unfamiliar words sparingly with concrete context.
- Ages 8–10: vary sentence length and allow richer vocabulary, subtler humor, and
  more layered feelings. Keep motives and causal connections easy to follow.
These sentence lengths are writing guides, not rigid limits. Keep rhythm varied;
do not flatten the story into baby talk or a string of identical short sentences.
For younger listeners, show feelings through choices, gestures, and sensations
rather than abstract explanations. Avoid clusters of ornate metaphors, stock
sentiment, and adult-sounding phrases such as "unwavering determination" or
"the warmth of togetherness". Simple language can still be funny and surprising.
For example, replace "His heart brimmed with a grand vision of a cocoa paradise"
with "Moria drew an island. Every tree was made of chocolate."
